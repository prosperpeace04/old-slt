01 Qualitative Test for Carbohydrates (Molisch's Test, Benedict's Test, Iodine Test)
▾
Aim

To carry out qualitative tests for the identification of carbohydrates in given samples using Molisch's test, Benedict's test, and Iodine test.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Test tubes and test tube rack
  • Dropper / Pasteur pipette
  • Water bath / Bunsen burner
  • Molisch's reagent (alpha-naphthol in ethanol)
  • Concentrated sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)
  • Benedict's reagent
  • Iodine solution (KI/I₂)
  • Glucose solution, starch solution, sucrose solution, distilled water
Procedure

Molisch's Test:

  1. Add 2 ml of each sample solution into separate test tubes.
  2. Add 2 drops of Molisch's reagent to each test tube and mix gently.
  3. Carefully add 1 ml of concentrated H₂SO₄ down the side of each test tube to form a layer.
  4. Observe for the formation of a purple/violet ring at the interface.

Benedict's Test:

  1. Add 2 ml of Benedict's reagent to each test tube.
  2. Add 0.5 ml of each sample solution.
  3. Heat in a boiling water bath for 5 minutes and observe colour change.

Iodine Test:

  1. Place 1 ml of each sample on a white tile or in a test tube.
  2. Add 2–3 drops of iodine solution and observe colour change.
Observation
SampleMolisch's TestBenedict's TestIodine Test
GlucosePurple ring ✓Brick-red precipitate ✓No colour change
StarchPurple ring ✓No colour changeBlue-black colour ✓
SucrosePurple ring ✓No colour changeNo colour change
Distilled WaterNo ringNo changeYellow-brown
Conclusion

The Molisch's test confirmed the presence of carbohydrates in glucose, starch, and sucrose solutions. Benedict's test identified glucose as a reducing sugar, evidenced by the brick-red precipitate. The iodine test confirmed the presence of starch by producing a characteristic blue-black colour. These qualitative tests are reliable and widely used for carbohydrate identification.

02 Qualitative Test for Proteins (Biuret Test, Xanthoproteic Test, Ninhydrin Test)
▾
Aim

To perform qualitative tests for the identification of proteins using the Biuret test, Xanthoproteic test, and Ninhydrin test.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Test tubes and test tube rack
  • Dropper / Pasteur pipette
  • Biuret reagent (NaOH + CuSO₄)
  • Concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃)
  • Ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH)
  • Ninhydrin solution (0.1%)
  • Egg albumin solution, gelatin solution, distilled water
  • Water bath / Bunsen burner
Procedure

Biuret Test:

  1. Add 1 ml of sample solution to a test tube.
  2. Add 1 ml of 10% NaOH solution and mix.
  3. Add 3 drops of 1% CuSO₄ solution and observe colour.

Xanthoproteic Test:

  1. Add 1 ml of sample to a test tube.
  2. Add 0.5 ml of concentrated HNO₃ carefully and heat gently.
  3. Cool and add excess NH₄OH. Observe colour change.

Ninhydrin Test:

  1. Add 1 ml of sample to a test tube.
  2. Add 3 drops of ninhydrin solution.
  3. Heat in a water bath for 5 minutes and observe.
Observation
SampleBiuret TestXanthoproteicNinhydrin
Egg albuminViolet/purple colour ✓Yellow → Orange ✓Purple colour ✓
GelatinViolet/purple colour ✓Yellow → Orange ✓Purple colour ✓
Distilled waterNo colour changeNo colour changeNo colour change
Conclusion

All three tests confirmed the presence of proteins in egg albumin and gelatin solutions. The Biuret test detected peptide bonds, the Xanthoproteic test detected aromatic amino acids, and the Ninhydrin test detected free amino groups. Distilled water showed negative results in all tests, confirming the specificity of the reagents used.

03 Qualitative Test for Lipids (Sudan III Test, Emulsification Test, Grease Spot Test)
▾
Aim

To identify the presence of lipids in given samples using Sudan III test, emulsification test, and grease spot test.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Test tubes and rack
  • Sudan III dye solution
  • Ethanol (absolute)
  • Distilled water
  • Cooking oil / groundnut oil
  • Filter / brown paper
  • Dropper
Procedure

Sudan III Test:

  1. Add 2 ml of sample to a test tube.
  2. Add 3 drops of Sudan III solution and shake gently.
  3. Observe for red/orange-stained droplets.

Emulsification Test:

  1. Add 2 ml of sample to 2 ml absolute ethanol. Mix well.
  2. Pour mixture into 2 ml distilled water.
  3. Observe for a white milky emulsion.

Grease Spot Test:

  1. Place a drop of sample on filter paper.
  2. Allow to dry and hold up to light.
  3. Observe for a translucent grease spot.
Observation
SampleSudan IIIEmulsificationGrease Spot
Cooking oilRed/orange droplets ✓White milky emulsion ✓Translucent spot ✓
Distilled waterDye sinks, no stainingNo emulsionNo translucent spot
Conclusion

The tests confirmed the presence of lipids in cooking oil. Sudan III stained the fat droplets red/orange, the emulsification test produced a characteristic milky emulsion, and the grease spot test left a permanent translucent mark. Distilled water produced negative results in all tests, validating the specificity of each test for lipid detection.

04 Determination of Vitamin C Content (Titration with DCPIP)
▾
Aim

To determine the Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) content of a fruit juice sample by titration with 2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol (DCPIP) solution.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Burette, pipette, conical flask
  • DCPIP solution (0.1%)
  • Fresh orange juice / lemon juice
  • Metaphosphoric acid (HPO₃) — stabilising agent
  • Distilled water
  • Standard ascorbic acid solution (1 mg/ml)
Procedure
  1. Prepare standard ascorbic acid solution and use it to standardise the DCPIP solution.
  2. Pipette 5 ml of the fruit juice into a conical flask.
  3. Add 5 ml of metaphosphoric acid to prevent oxidation.
  4. Fill the burette with DCPIP solution.
  5. Titrate the fruit juice against DCPIP, swirling continuously.
  6. Record the volume of DCPIP used when the pink colour persists for 15 seconds (endpoint).
  7. Repeat for accuracy and calculate Vitamin C content.
Observation
TitrationInitial Burette Reading (ml)Final Burette Reading (ml)Volume DCPIP Used (ml)
1st (rough)0.002.802.80
2nd0.002.602.60
3rd0.002.652.65
Average——2.625 ml
Conclusion

The titration successfully determined the Vitamin C content of the fruit juice. The average DCPIP volume used was 2.625 ml. The endpoint was identified by the persistence of a pink/rose colour, indicating complete oxidation of ascorbic acid. This method demonstrates that DCPIP titration is a simple, effective technique for quantitative determination of Vitamin C in food samples.

01Monohybrid Cross Using Corn Seeds (Ratio Verification)
▾
Aim

To verify Mendel's Law of Segregation by observing and counting phenotypic ratios of corn seed traits.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Corn cob with mixed seed colours (yellow and purple)
  • Counting tray
  • Marker and data sheet
Procedure
  1. Obtain a corn cob that is a product of a monohybrid cross (F2 generation).
  2. Count the number of yellow seeds and purple seeds on the cob.
  3. Record the numbers and calculate the ratio of yellow to purple seeds.
  4. Compare the observed ratio to the expected 3:1 Mendelian ratio.
Observation
Seed ColourCountPercentage
Yellow (dominant)37274.7%
Purple (recessive)12625.3%
Total498100%
Conclusion

The observed ratio of yellow to purple seeds was approximately 2.95:1, which closely approximates the expected 3:1 Mendelian ratio. This confirms Mendel's Law of Segregation — alleles separate during gamete formation and reunite randomly at fertilisation, producing predictable phenotypic ratios in offspring.

03Construction and Analysis of a Pedigree Chart
▾
Aim

To construct a pedigree chart from given family data and determine the pattern of inheritance of a given trait.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Graph paper or plain paper
  • Pencil, ruler, eraser
  • Family data showing affected and unaffected individuals across three generations
Procedure
  1. Using standard pedigree symbols (squares for males, circles for females, filled shapes for affected individuals), draw Generation I at the top.
  2. Connect parents with a horizontal line and offspring with a vertical line dropping to a horizontal sibship line.
  3. Continue for Generations II and III based on the given data.
  4. Analyse the pattern to determine if the trait is dominant, recessive, autosomal, or sex-linked.
Observation

The pedigree showed that the trait skipped Generation II in some branches but reappeared in Generation III, suggesting a recessive pattern of inheritance. Both males and females were affected equally, suggesting autosomal inheritance rather than sex-linked.

Conclusion

The constructed pedigree chart revealed an autosomal recessive pattern of inheritance. Unaffected carriers in Generation II passed the recessive allele to affected offspring in Generation III. Pedigree analysis is a valuable tool in medical genetics for tracking inherited traits across generations.

04Preparation and Observation of Mitosis in Onion Root Tips
▾
Aim

To prepare a temporary slide of onion root tip cells and identify the stages of mitosis under the microscope.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Onion root tips (2–3 days germinated)
  • Hydrochloric acid (1M HCl)
  • Aceto-orcein or Feulgen stain
  • Microscope, glass slides, coverslips
  • Scalpel, forceps, water bath
Procedure
  1. Cut 1 cm of onion root tip and place in 1M HCl at 60°C for 5 minutes to soften cell walls.
  2. Rinse with distilled water and stain with aceto-orcein for 10 minutes.
  3. Place the root tip on a slide, add a drop of stain, and gently squash with a coverslip.
  4. Observe under low and high power magnification and identify cells in interphase, prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase.
Observation

Cells in various stages of mitosis were observed. The majority of cells were in interphase (chromatin spread). Prophase cells showed condensed chromosomes. Metaphase cells showed chromosomes aligned at the equatorial plate. Anaphase showed chromosomes being pulled apart. Telophase showed two forming nuclei.

Conclusion

The preparation of onion root tip squash slides successfully demonstrated the stages of mitosis. The actively dividing meristematic cells of the root tip made it an ideal material for this experiment. Mitosis ensures identical genetic material is distributed to daughter cells, maintaining chromosome number.

01Quadrat Sampling Method — Estimation of Population Density
▾
Aim

To estimate the population density of plant species in a given habitat using the quadrat sampling method.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Quadrat frame (1m × 1m)
  • Measuring tape and pegs
  • Data recording sheet
  • Identification chart for plant species
Procedure
  1. Select the study area and randomly throw the quadrat frame at least 10 times within the area.
  2. For each quadrat placement, identify and count all plant species within the frame.
  3. Record results in a table.
  4. Calculate population density: Density = Total number of individuals ÷ Total area sampled.
Observation
SpeciesTotal Count (10 quadrats)Density (per m²)
Grass (Pennisetum sp.)14814.8
Sedge434.3
Broad-leaf weed272.7
Conclusion

The quadrat method provided reliable estimates of plant population density in the study area. Grass was the dominant species with a density of 14.8 individuals/m². The method is simple, reproducible, and widely used in ecological field studies for estimating plant abundance and distribution.

02Mark-Recapture Method — Estimation of Animal Population Size
▾
Aim

To estimate the population size of a mobile animal species using the Lincoln-Petersen mark-recapture method.

Apparatus / Materials
  • Traps or nets
  • Non-toxic marking paint or tags
  • Data sheet
  • Container for holding captured animals
Procedure
  1. On Day 1: Capture as many animals as possible. Mark each one and release back into the habitat.
  2. On Day 2: Recapture a sample from the same area. Count total captured and how many are marked.
  3. Apply the formula: N = (M × C) ÷ R, where M = marked in first capture, C = total in second capture, R = recaptured marked individuals.
Observation
VariableValue
Marked individuals (M)40
Second capture total (C)35
Recaptured marked (R)14
Estimated population (N)100
Conclusion

Using the Lincoln-Petersen formula, the estimated population size was 100 individuals. The mark-recapture method is effective for estimating populations of mobile animals that cannot be directly counted. Accuracy depends on the assumption that marked individuals mix randomly with the population before recapture.
